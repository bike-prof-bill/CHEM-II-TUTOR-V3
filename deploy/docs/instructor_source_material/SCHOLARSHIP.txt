## 6. What others are doing

### The evidence for AI tutoring is real and better than expected

A Harvard randomized controlled trial (Kestin et al., 2025, ~500 undergraduates)
found that students using a purpose-built AI tutor learned more, in less time,
than students in an active-learning physics class — a demanding comparison,
since active learning is itself the evidence-based standard. A three-arm RCT
with 362 nursing students in anatomy found module scores of 82.5 for an
AI-enhanced group against 77.6 blended and 74.1 traditional. Meta-analyses
report large positive effects, with the strongest results where the AI tutor
replaces rather than supplements instruction.

This is not a fringe result. It is a reasonably robust finding across countries
and subjects.

### But the mechanism matters enormously, and this is the key finding

Bastani et al. (2025) ran the comparison that should shape every institutional
decision here. Students using an **unguarded** chatbot scored higher while
practicing — and **lower on a later test taken without it** than students who
never had access at all. A **Socratically guarded** version of the same
underlying model kept the practice gain and eliminated the later loss.

Read that again. The unguarded tool did not merely fail to help. It produced
measurable harm to durable learning while producing the appearance of help.

A parallel literature on **metacognitive laziness** (Fan et al., 2025, *BJET*;
Yurt & Kuşci, 2026) finds that generative AI use is associated with lower
critical-thinking dispositions, mediated by metacognitive weakness and what the
authors call epistemic laziness. Because AI output is fluent and immediate,
students interpret ease of access as evidence of understanding.

The operative distinction in this literature is **augmentation versus
automation** — whether the tool extends the student's judgment or substitutes
for their effort. Fluency is not the variable. Whether the student has to think
is.

**This is the single strongest argument for the design described above**, and
also the sharpest reason to be sceptical of institutional deployments that hand
students an unconstrained chatbot and call it tutoring.

### The field is converging on answer-withholding as the core technical problem

Recent benchmarks — MathTutorBench, EduBench, Google's LearnLM rubrics —
explicitly measure "answer leakage" as a negative and score tutors on whether
they ask a question the student must answer before progressing.

A significant line of work addresses "scaffolding collapse": a capable model,
pressed by a frustrated student, does not reliably withhold on the strength of a
prompt alone. Published architectures use a non-LLM policy core reading trusted
learner state, deterministic detectors, and separate judge models.

**Our design converges on the same conclusion from a different direction.**
The server-side verification layer is a non-LLM policy core. Milestone state
lives on the server, not in the conversation. The model is handed a verdict it
cannot override. This is not novel — it is the emerging consensus — but it
means the approach is aligned with where the field is going rather than with
where consumer chatbots are.

### Institutional deployments are expensive and adoption is the real problem

Arizona State's partnership with OpenAI was the first of its kind. At list price
for ChatGPT Enterprise, universal access for ASU's 145,000 students would cost
roughly $99 million annually — about 11% of the university's operating budget.
Access is consequently allocated by proposal. Per-student AI tutoring
subscriptions run $4–10 per month; at scale, that is $200,000–500,000 monthly
for a large district.

**The adoption numbers are the sobering part.** Khan Academy's Khanmigo, the
most heavily resourced purpose-built AI tutor in existence, reports that **only
about 15% of students open the tool at all**. Two million Khanmigo users against
ChatGPT's roughly 140 million weekly math queries. Students are already using
generative AI heavily — they are largely not using the pedagogically designed
version.

The Stanford AI Index 2026 reports over 80% of US students using AI for
schoolwork while only 6% of teachers say their institution's AI policies are
clear.

**The implication for us is uncomfortable.** A carefully guarded tutor competes
against an unguarded one the student already has open in another tab, which
answers instantly and never says no. Our design advantage is exactly what makes
it less attractive in the moment. Any honest projection has to account for that,
and the most likely failure of this project is not that it teaches badly — it is
that students do not use it.

## 7. The competing-tab problem

Every faculty member reading this will ask the same question, so it is worth
addressing directly: what stops a student from pasting the milestone-five
question into an unguarded chatbot in another tab?

**Nothing does, and no technical measure will.** Timed answer windows do not
work — a general-purpose model answers in under five seconds, so any window
generous enough to type in is generous enough to paste into. They are defeated
by a second device. They conflict with extended-time accommodations. And they
apply pressure to precisely the reflective step the design exists to elicit.

Worse, **the stage the tutor can verify is the stage a student has least reason
to outsource.** Arithmetic is checked deterministically. The explanation that
carries the credit is the one no automated layer can check — and it is exactly
the kind of question a general-purpose model answers well.

This is not a solved problem anywhere in the field. What follows is what appears
to actually help.

**Assess without the tool.** Grades come from examinations, where the tutor is
not present. This is both the incentive structure and the validity check: a
student who clears every milestone and then cannot perform on the exam has told
the instructor something specific, per student and per topic. No cheating
detection apparatus is required — only the outcome measure that should be
collected anyway.

**Personalize the final question to the session.** The system holds the full
transcript. A question built from the student's own work — *you first got 79
g/mol and then corrected to 36,600; what changed, and why does the second make
sense for a protein when the first does not* — cannot be answered by an external
model without handing it the conversation. This is a stronger question
pedagogically whether or not anyone is circumventing anything.

**Require articulation before help.** SocraticAI at Ashoka University requires
students to state their current understanding and prior attempts before the
system responds at all, with a validation layer rejecting thin submissions. Over
a three-week deployment they observed question quality shift from *"my code
doesn't work"* to *"I implemented recursion correctly, but I'm unsure how my
base case terminates."* Roughly 75% of students produced substantive
reflections; instructors reported fewer low-value office-hours questions.

That report is observational — no control group, no stated sample size, three
weeks, and **no learning outcomes measured**. It should be read as a
well-designed intervention rather than as evidence. But the front gate is the
one thing an unguarded chatbot structurally cannot do: it never asks the student
what they already think.

**Make the guarded path the easier path.** The tutor knows which problem is
loaded, where the student is, and what they got wrong. An external model needs
the whole problem pasted in. Where the purpose-built tool is genuinely faster
for the task at hand, use follows without enforcement.

**The honest summary:** this is a motivation problem wearing a security costume.
Every barrier is defeated by a second tab. What works is making the guarded path
more useful and making the unguarded path visibly fail to pay off at assessment
time.

## 8. What would make this credible

Roughly in order of value:

1. **A control comparison.** Even a quasi-experimental one — sections with and
   without, matched on prior performance. Without this, everything above is
   argument rather than evidence.
2. **Adoption data.** The logs already contain it. What fraction of enrolled
   students open the tool? How many return? Where do sessions terminate?
3. **A transfer measure.** Bastani's design is the right one: assess without the
   tool present. Practice-time performance is the wrong outcome.
4. **A second instructor.** Both for sustainability and because it would reveal
   how much of the design encodes one person's pedagogy versus something
   general.
5. **A FERPA and policy review**, before any use beyond a single course.
6. **Automated regression testing.** The verification layer needs its own tests
   that run on every change. The bugs found during development were found by
   hand.

---

## 9. Summary judgment

The architecture is sound and is aligned with where the research field is
converging. The pedagogical design targets a real and well-documented failure
mode of AI-assisted learning. The deterministic verification layer is the right
answer to a problem that most deployments have not yet acknowledged they have.

It is also a single-instructor project with no outcomes evidence, a bus factor
of one, an unreviewed privacy posture, and a recent history of silently
mis-grading students. It is a promising prototype and a plausible research
instrument. It is not yet a validated intervention, and it should not be
described as one.

One positioning note. The published systems closest to this one — SocraticAI at
Ashoka, the CS50 deployment at Harvard, the scaffolding-collapse literature —
have sophisticated interaction protocols and **no deterministic verification
layer at all**. They trust the model to judge correctness. This project has the
verifier they lack and lacks the interaction protocol they have. The two are
complementary rather than competing, which is a more useful thing to say to a
funder or a collaborator than a claim of novelty.

The most useful thing faculty in other disciplines could take from it is not the
tool. It is the design principle: **when an AI system makes a claim your
discipline can check deterministically, check it deterministically, and do not
let the model be the judge of its own output.**
